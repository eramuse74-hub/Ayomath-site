  • Order: sine/cosine → radians → tangent → exponentials → calculus → complex numbers → $e^{j\theta}$. Later ones lean on earlier ones.

Sine and cosine — what they actually are

Not ratios in a triangle. The two coordinates of a point going round a circle.

1. The definition that makes everything else follow

  • Draw a circle of radius 1, centred at the origin.
  • Put a point on it at angle $\theta$, measured counter-clockwise from the positive horizontal axis.
  • $\cos\theta$ = the point's horizontal coordinate.
  • $\sin\theta$ = the point's vertical coordinate. That is the whole definition.
  • Picture it as shadows: light from above casts the point's shadow on the horizontal axis at $\cos\theta$; light from the side casts it on the vertical axis at $\sin\theta$.
  • The school version, "opposite over hypotenuse", is the same picture with hypotenuse 1. The circle version is better because it keeps working past $90^\circ$, for negative angles, and for angles that grow forever — which is what an angle does when something rotates.
Try: drag the point. Watch the two shadows. Put it at $90^\circ$ — the horizontal shadow vanishes ($\cos 90^\circ = 0$).

2. Facts you can now read off instead of memorising

  • Both stay between $-1$ and $1$. A point on a unit circle is never further than 1 from the centre.
  • $\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1$. Pythagoras: the point is at distance 1 from the origin.
  • $\cos(-\theta) = \cos\theta$, $\sin(-\theta) = -\sin\theta$. A negative angle is the mirror image below the axis: same horizontal position, opposite height.
  • $\sin(\pi - \theta) = \sin\theta$. Mirror across the vertical axis: same height. This is why $\sin x = \tfrac12$ has two solutions.
  • Cosine is sine a quarter turn early: $\cos\theta = \sin(\theta + \pi/2)$. Same wave, shifted. Remember this: a quarter turn is what $j$ will turn out to be.
  • Reference angle + quadrant. Size comes from the acute angle to the horizontal axis (only $30^\circ/45^\circ/60^\circ$ values needed); sign comes from the quadrant.

3. From angle to time: the wave

  • Let the point go round at a steady rate $\omega$ radians per second, so $\theta = \omega t$.
  • Plot its height against time: that is the sine wave.
  • Plot its horizontal position against time: that is the cosine wave.
  • The figure below does exactly this — circle on the left, height unrolled to the right, horizontal position unrolled downward.
Try: press play. Then pause at $\theta = \pi/2$ and $\theta = \pi$ and read the two shadows.
The sentence to make obvious: a sinusoid is not a shape somebody drew. It is what uniform rotation looks like when you only see one coordinate.
  • Mains voltage: a coil turning in a magnetic field → sinusoid at 50/60 Hz.
  • Mass on a spring, small pendulum swings, pressure of a pure tone: all "a point going round, seen from the side".
  • MRI: the magnetisation literally precesses (rotates); the received signal is this picture.

4. Three knobs: amplitude, frequency, phase

The general sinusoid is $A\sin(\omega t + \phi)$. Three numbers, three independent knobs:

  • $A$ — how tall. The radius of the circle.
  • $\omega$ — how fast, in rad/s. One turn is $2\pi$ rad, so period $T = 2\pi/\omega$ and frequency $f = \omega/2\pi$.
  • $\phi$ — where it starts at $t = 0$. Shifts the wave sideways in time by $\phi/\omega$; changes neither height nor speed.
  • Two sinusoids of the same $\omega$ differ only in $A$ and $\phi$: a length and an angle. That pair is one complex number — the whole trick of AC circuit analysis (modules 1 and 6).

5. Why engineers are obsessed with them

  • Differentiate a sine → a cosine: same shape, a quarter turn earlier, scaled by $\omega$.
  • Integrate → same shape, a quarter turn later.
  • Add two sinusoids of the same frequency → a sinusoid of that frequency.
  • Therefore any circuit of resistors, capacitors and inductors, fed a sinusoid, gives out a sinusoid of the same frequency. Only amplitude and phase change. No other waveform does this.
  • That is why sinusoids are the alphabet: every reasonable signal is a sum of them (Fourier), and a filter is just "what it does to each frequency".
Where you will meet this
  • ECE 230: sinusoidal steady state, phasors, impedance, AC power.
  • BME 310 / 462: amplifiers and filters are specified by what they do to each frequency; ECG and EMG are analysed by frequency content.
  • ECE 330: Fourier series and transforms — every signal as sinusoids.
  • Imaging: MRI signal is a rotating magnetisation; ultrasound is a pressure sinusoid.

Radians — why π keeps appearing

Degrees are a human convention. Radians are what the circle itself measures.

1. What a radian is

  • Take a circle of radius $r$. Walk along the rim a distance of exactly $r$.
  • The angle you turned through is one radian (about $57.3^\circ$).
  • The whole rim is $2\pi r$ long, so a full turn is $2\pi$ radians. That is the only reason $\pi$ appears: angle in radians = arc length ÷ radius.
  • Conversions: $360^\circ = 2\pi$, $180^\circ = \pi$, $90^\circ = \pi/2$; in general $\theta_{rad} = \theta_{deg}\cdot\pi/180$.
  • Arc length $s = r\theta$ — only in radians. The first of many formulas that silently assume radians.

2. Why calculus refuses to work in degrees

  • Near $\theta = 0$ the point's height is almost exactly its arc length: $\sin\theta \approx \theta$.
  • So in radians the slope of $\sin$ at the origin is exactly $1$, and $\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta = \cos\theta$ comes out clean.
  • In degrees every derivative drags a factor $\pi/180 \approx 0.01745$ along: $\frac{d}{dx}\sin x^\circ = 0.01745\cos x^\circ$. Nobody wants that.
  • So calculus, physics and every course after this one use radians without saying so.
Try: drag $x_0$ toward $0$. The tangent line becomes $y = x$. That is $\sin x \approx x$ — a radian statement.

3. Angular frequency

  • A point rotating at $\omega$ rad/s has turned through angle $\omega t$ after $t$ seconds — an angle you can feed straight into $\sin$, $\cos$ or $e^{j\theta}$.
  • That is why circuits use $\omega = 2\pi f$: it saves writing $2\pi$ in every formula.
  • In $e^{j\theta}$ the exponent is always radians.
The sentence to make obvious: $\pi$ is not a unit. It is the length of half a rim measured in radii.
Where you will meet this
  • $\omega = 2\pi f$ in every circuits and signals formula; $\omega t$ as an angle.
  • Calculator-mode errors on exams: an answer off by a factor near $57$ or $0.017$ is this.
  • Small-angle approximation in optics, mechanics and control ($\sin\theta \approx \theta$ linearises a pendulum).

Tangent, slope and the quadrant problem

tan θ is the slope of the radius line. That explains everything odd about it.

1. What tangent is

  • $\tan\theta = \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}$ of the line from the origin to the point — its slope.
  • Blows up at $90^\circ$: a vertical line has no finite slope ($\cos = 0$).
  • Repeats every $180^\circ$, not $360^\circ$: the line at $\theta$ and at $\theta + 180^\circ$ is the same line. The point moved to the other end; a line has one slope.
  • Sign: positive in quadrants I and III (both coordinates same sign), negative in II and IV.
Try: drag the point through $90^\circ$ and watch the tan readout blow up and flip sign. Then compare $36.87^\circ$ with $216.87^\circ$: same tan.

2. The quadrant problem (it follows you into every complex-number calculation)

  • Because tan repeats every half turn, going backwards is ambiguous: slope $0.75$ could be the point at $36.87^\circ$ or at $216.87^\circ$.
  • Your calculator's $\arctan$ always returns the answer between $-90^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ (quadrants I and IV).
  • If your point is really in quadrant II or III, add or subtract $180^\circ$ yourself.
  • Programming languages fix it with $\operatorname{atan2}(y, x)$: given both coordinates separately, it can see the signs and returns the true angle.
  • Example: $-3 - 4j$. $\arctan(4/3) = 53.13^\circ$; the point is in quadrant III; so the angle is $233.13^\circ$ (or $-126.87^\circ$).
The sentence to make obvious: $\arctan$ knows the slope; it cannot know which end of the line you are on. You have to look.
Where you will meet this
  • Every polar conversion in module 1, every phase angle in ECE 230, every phase plot in ECE 330.
  • Bode phase plots wrapping at $\pm180^\circ$ — this ambiguity showing up on a graph.

Exponentials and logarithms — time constants

e^x is the function whose rate of change equals its current value. Everything about it follows from that.

1. What $e$ is for

  • Many things change at a rate proportional to how much there is: a population, compound interest, charge draining from a capacitor, undecayed nuclei, a cooling body's temperature difference.
  • "Rate proportional to amount" is the equation $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = k\,y$. Its solution is $y = y_0 e^{kt}$.
  • $e \approx 2.718$ is simply the base for which the proportionality constant is exactly $1$: $\frac{d}{dt}e^{t} = e^{t}$.
  • Any other base works but drags a factor along ($\frac{d}{dt}2^t = 0.693\cdot 2^t$) — exactly as degrees drag $\pi/180$ along in trigonometry.

2. Decay and the time constant

  • Discharging capacitor: $v(t) = V_0 e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = RC$.
  • Read the exponent as "how many time constants have passed".
  • After $1\tau$: $e^{-1} = 37\%$ left. After $2\tau$: $13.5\%$. After $5\tau$: under $1\%$ — engineers call that "done".
  • The curve never reaches zero; it loses the same fraction per $\tau$, not the same amount.
  • The tangent's slope is always $-(\text{current value})/\tau$: steepness proportional to height — the defining property, in picture form.
  • Same curve with a positive exponent is growth. A charging capacitor is $V(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$: the gap closing exponentially.
  • Preview of module 4: $e^{st}$ with a complex $s$ is decay and rotation at once — a damped oscillation.
Try: drag the point along the decay. Check that at $x = 2$ (one time constant) the height is $37\%$ of $5$, and that slope = −height/2 everywhere.

3. Logarithms: the question "what exponent?"

  • $\ln y$ answers: $e$ to what power gives $y$? It undoes the exponential.
  • Solving for time: $V_0 e^{-t/\tau} = v \Rightarrow t = \tau\ln(V_0/v)$ — "how many time constants does this ratio take".
  • Log rules are exponent rules read backwards:
    • $e^{a}e^{b} = e^{a+b}$ ⇒ $\ln(PQ) = \ln P + \ln Q$: multiplication becomes addition.
    • $(e^{a})^n = e^{na}$ ⇒ $\ln(P^n) = n\ln P$: powers become multipliers.
    • Other bases: $\log_b y = \ln y / \ln b$. Base 10 is for decibels; base 2 counts doublings (bits).
  • "Multiplication becomes addition" is why decibels exist: stage gains multiply, dB values add, and a Bode plot (dB against log frequency) turns each stage into straight lines you can sketch.
  • Whenever a quantity spans many orders of magnitude (1 Hz to 1 MHz; 1 µV to 1 V), engineers plot the log.
The sentence to make obvious: exponentials count time in time-constants; logarithms count how many time-constants a ratio is worth.
Where you will meet this
  • ECE 230: RC and RL transients, $\tau = RC$ and $L/R$; every first-order circuit.
  • BME 310 / 462: filter cut-offs, decibels, Bode plots, amplifier dynamic range.
  • Pharmacokinetics, radioactive tracers, Nernst potential (a log), cell growth.

Derivative and integral — slope and accumulation

A derivative is a slope. An integral is an accumulated total. Every rule is bookkeeping on top of those two pictures.

1. The derivative is a slope at an instant

  • Zoom in on any smooth curve far enough and it looks straight.
  • $f'(x_0)$ is the slope of that straight line — the tangent — at $x_0$.
  • It is a rate: how fast $f$ is changing right there, in units of $f$ per unit of $x$.
  • It is not the value of $f$ (that is the height) and not the total change over an interval (that is a difference of heights).
  • Slope is zero at every peak and trough — that is why "set the derivative to zero" finds maxima and minima.
Try: type any function into the box and drag. Find the places where the slope readout is $0$; find where it is largest.

2. The rules are about how rates combine

  • Chain rule — rates multiply along a chain. If $y$ changes 3× as fast as $u$, and $u$ changes 2× as fast as $x$, then $y$ changes 6× as fast as $x$: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\frac{du}{dx}$. In $\sin(x^2)$: outer rate $\cos(x^2)$, inner rate $2x$, multiply.
  • Product rule — two things changing, two reasons for growth. A rectangle $u \times v$: widen by $du$ and you add a strip $v\,du$; heighten by $dv$ and you add a strip $u\,dv$. So $d(uv) = u\,dv + v\,du$.
  • Sine and cosine. $\frac{d}{dt}\sin\omega t = \omega\cos\omega t$: same shape, a quarter turn early, scaled by $\omega$. Faster oscillation, steeper slopes. This becomes "differentiation is multiplication by $j\omega$" in module 1.

3. The integral is accumulation, and it undoes the derivative

  • $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is the signed area under $f$ from $a$ to $b$.
  • More usefully: the total accumulated when $f$ is a rate — distance from velocity, charge from current ($q = \int i\,dt$), energy from power.
  • Fundamental theorem: accumulating a rate recovers the quantity. If $F' = f$ then $\int_a^b f\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$.
  • So to integrate, ask "what has this as its derivative?" — then check by differentiating your answer. Not optional; it catches every sign and factor slip.
  • Two habits worth more than any technique: sketch before integrating (you then know the sign and rough size), and differentiate every antiderivative before moving on.

4. Why this is the language of circuits

  • A capacitor stores charge; current is the rate of charge flow: $i = C\,\dfrac{dv}{dt}$.
  • An inductor resists changes in current: $v = L\,\dfrac{di}{dt}$.
  • Kirchhoff's laws then give equations containing derivatives — differential equations. Every RC, RL or RLC response is the solution of one.
  • Module 4 is about those solutions; module 1 is about the trick that turns them into algebra for sinusoids.
The sentence to make obvious: derivative = slope of the tangent = rate now. Integral = area = total so far. Each undoes the other.
Where you will meet this
  • ECE 230: capacitor and inductor equations, first- and second-order circuits.
  • ECE 330: convolution and the Fourier transform are integrals.
  • ECE 332: controllers literally called P, I and D — proportional, integral, derivative.
  • Physiology models: flows are derivatives of volumes; concentrations are integrals of flows.

Complex numbers — why they were worth inventing

Not "imaginary". A second axis, so that numbers can have a direction as well as a size.

1. The problem that forced them

  • $x^2 - 4x + 13 = 0$ has no real solution: the parabola never touches the axis.
  • The quadratic formula still produces something: $x = 2 \pm \sqrt{-9}$. For centuries that was declared meaningless.
  • The productive move: allow $\sqrt{-1}$, call it $j$ (engineers; mathematicians say $i$), and see what happens.
  • What happens: every polynomial then has exactly as many roots as its degree, and the "missing" roots sit just off the real line as a mirror pair.
Try: raise $c$ slowly. Watch the two roots slide together on the axis, meet, and then leave it vertically as a pair.

2. The picture that makes $j$ obvious

  • Real numbers on a horizontal line, as usual.
  • Multiplying by $-1$ flips a number to the other side: a half turn about the origin.
  • What operation, done twice, is a half turn? A quarter turn.
  • So let $j$ = "rotate a quarter turn counter-clockwise". Then $j\cdot j$ = half turn = $-1$. That is all $j^2 = -1$ says.
  • The imaginary axis is where the real axis goes after a quarter turn. A complex number $a + bj$ is a point (an arrow) in the resulting plane.
  • Two names for the same arrow: its two shadows ($a$, $b$ — rectangular form) or its length and direction ($r$, $\theta$ — polar form).
  • Rectangular is for adding (tip to tail). Polar is for multiplying — see next.
Try: drag the point and watch both names update. Put it on the imaginary axis: that is $bj$, angle $90^\circ$.

3. Multiplication is rotate-and-stretch

  • Multiply two complex numbers: lengths multiply, angles add.
  • Multiply by $j$ (length 1, angle $90^\circ$): a pure quarter turn.
  • Multiply by $2$ (length 2, angle 0): a pure stretch.
  • Multiply by $2j$: both.
  • Everything in module 1 follows: powers rotate repeatedly (a spiral); roots divide the angle (so there are $n$ of them, equally spaced); the conjugate is the mirror image (so $z\bar z$ is real); $1/z$ is "undo the rotation, invert the stretch".
Try: discover the rule yourself before opening the check under the figure. Put $z_2$ on the unit circle first, so only the angle changes.

4. Why an engineer needs a number with a direction

  • Recall the three knobs of a sinusoid: amplitude, frequency, phase.
  • In a circuit driven at one frequency, every voltage and current shares that frequency — so each is fully described by amplitude and phase: a length and an angle: a complex number.
  • A resistor keeps the phase. A capacitor or inductor shifts it by a quarter turn — multiplication by $\mp j$.
  • So impedance is a complex number, Ohm's law becomes $V = ZI$ with complex $Z$, and a circuit that needed differential equations is solved with algebra.
  • That is module 6 — the first place this pays off in your actual coursework.
The sentence to make obvious: a complex number is a length and a direction. Multiplying rotates and stretches. $j$ is a quarter turn.
Where you will meet this
  • ECE 230: phasors, impedance, complex power — every AC problem.
  • ECE 330: the Fourier transform lives in the complex plane; poles and zeros are complex numbers.
  • ECE 332: root locus is literally "where the roots move in this plane".
  • MRI: the received signal is complex-valued by nature (two quadrature channels).

$e^{j\theta}$ — the one identity that runs the whole chain

Exponential growth with an imaginary rate is rotation.

1. What it says

  • $e^{j\theta} = \cos\theta + j\sin\theta$.
  • In words: $e^{j\theta}$ is the point on the unit circle at angle $\theta$. Horizontal coordinate $\cos\theta$, vertical coordinate $\sin\theta$.
  • $\theta$ is in radians. Always.
Try: slide $\theta$ to $\pi$: the point is at $-1$, so $e^{j\pi} = -1$. Slide to $\pi/2$: the point is $j$.

2. Why it is true (the picture, not the series)

  • The exponential is the function whose rate of change equals its value: $\frac{d}{d\theta}e^{k\theta} = k\,e^{k\theta}$.
  • Put $k = j$. The velocity of the point $z = e^{j\theta}$ is $j$ × its position: its position vector turned a quarter turn, same length.
  • Velocity always perpendicular to position ⇒ the point can neither approach nor leave the origin ⇒ it stays on the circle of radius $|z(0)| = 1$.
  • Speed equal to radius ⇒ it turns at one radian per unit of $\theta$.
  • It starts at $e^0 = 1$. After parameter $\theta$ it has turned through $\theta$. That point is $\cos\theta + j\sin\theta$. Done.
  • The power-series proof reaches the same place by brute force: expand $e^{j\theta}$, use $j^2 = -1$; even terms assemble into $\cos$, odd terms into $j\sin$. The alternating signs in those series are the quarter-turn cycle $1, j, -1, -j$.

3. What it buys you

  • Polar form becomes an exponential: $r\angle\theta = re^{j\theta}$. Multiplication is $r_1 r_2\, e^{j(\theta_1 + \theta_2)}$ — lengths multiply, angles add, because exponents add.
  • Trig identities stop being memorised: $e^{j(A+B)} = e^{jA}e^{jB}$; multiply out the right side; real part is $\cos(A+B)$, imaginary part is $\sin(A+B)$. One line each.
  • Sinusoids become exponentials: $\cos\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta} + e^{-j\theta}}{2}$, $\sin\theta = \frac{e^{j\theta} - e^{-j\theta}}{2j}$. A real cosine is two counter-rotating points whose sideways parts cancel.
  • Differentiation becomes multiplication: $\frac{d}{dt}e^{j\omega t} = j\omega\,e^{j\omega t}$. For a phasor, "differentiate" = "multiply by $j\omega$", "integrate" = "divide by $j\omega$". A capacitor's $i = C\,dv/dt$ becomes $I = j\omega C\,V$: impedance $1/(j\omega C)$. Calculus has become algebra.
  • Damped oscillation is one exponential: $e^{st}$ with $s = \sigma + j\omega$ is $e^{\sigma t}$ (decay if $\sigma < 0$) × $e^{j\omega t}$ (rotation). Where $s$ sits in the plane is the behaviour: module 4, ECE 330's poles, ECE 332's root locus.
Try: a unit-length number raised to successive powers walks round the circle. $e^{j\theta}\cdot e^{j\theta} = e^{j2\theta}$: rotations compose by adding angles, exponentials by adding exponents — the same thing.
The sentence to make obvious: $e^{j\theta}$ is pure rotation. Everything from circuits to Fourier is that sentence wearing different clothes.
Where you will meet this
  • ECE 230: phasors are $e^{j\omega t}$ with the $e^{j\omega t}$ left unwritten.
  • ECE 330: Fourier transform $\int x(t)e^{-j\omega t}dt$ — winding a signal round the circle; Laplace's $e^{-st}$ generalises it.
  • ECE 332: $s$-plane, poles, stability — all positions of $e^{st}$ exponents.
